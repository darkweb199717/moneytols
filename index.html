How federal income tax brackets work

The US uses a progressive system with seven rates: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. A common myth is that moving into a higher bracket taxes all of your income at that higher rate. It does not. Each bracket only applies to the dollars that fall inside its range.

For example, a single filer with $60,000 of taxable income in 2026 pays 10% on the first $12,400, 12% on the amount between $12,400 and $50,400, and 22% only on the remaining $9,600. Their top (marginal) rate is 22%, but their effective rate — total tax divided by total income — is far lower.

Before brackets apply, you subtract either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions, plus any pre-tax payroll contributions such as a traditional 401(k) or HSA. Eligible tax credits, such as the Child Tax Credit, reduce federal income tax. This tool models a simplified nonrefundable CTC and refundable ACTC estimate, but does not verify every eligibility or filing-specific rule.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?

Your marginal rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of taxable income — useful for deciding whether to contribute more to a 401(k). Your effective federal rate is federal income tax after nonrefundable credits, divided by gross income. It is not a complete measure of total payroll and state taxes, and it may not always be lower than the marginal rate in every simplified scenario.

Should I take the standard deduction or itemize?

In general, compare your eligible itemized deductions with the standard deduction and use the applicable rules for your filing situation. This simplified tool lets you enter either a standard or itemized amount but does not determine full itemization eligibility. Itemizing usually only wins if you have a large mortgage interest, state and local tax, medical or charitable total.

Does this include Social Security and Medicare taxes?

Yes. Social Security is 6.2% of wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500. Medicare is 1.45% on all wages, plus an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on wages above $200,000 (single/HOH), $250,000 (joint) or $125,000 (married filing separately). Note that pre-tax items differ for FICA purposes: HSA contributions through payroll avoid FICA, while traditional 401(k) contributions do not.

Why is my state tax only an estimate?

States differ enormously: nine have no wage income tax, some are flat-rate, and others have their own brackets, deductions and credits. This tool applies a single representative rate per state to your federal taxable income to give you a ballpark figure, not a filing-ready number.

How do pre-tax deductions reduce my tax bill?

Traditional 401(k), 403(b), HSA and FSA contributions can reduce federal taxable income when eligible. Traditional 401(k) and 403(b) contributions generally remain subject to FICA, while eligible payroll HSA/FSA contributions may also avoid FICA. A $6,000 contribution for someone in the 22% bracket could reduce federal income tax by about $1,320, depending on eligibility and other details.

Which tax year does this calculator use?

The 2026 tax year, using the inflation-adjusted brackets, standard deductions and credit amounts published by the IRS in Revenue Procedure 2025-32. Some newer deductions, special situations, and eligibility rules are not fully modeled. These are the numbers for income earned during 2026 and reported on the return you file in 2027.

What is the 2026 standard deduction?

For the 2026 tax year the standard deduction is $16,100 for single filers and married filing separately, $32,200 for married filing jointly and qualifying surviving spouses, and $24,150 for heads of household. These amounts are adjusted each year for inflation by the IRS. If your itemized deductions exceed these amounts it may be worth itemizing instead.

How much will I get back as a tax refund?

A refund happens when the amount withheld from your paychecks during the year exceeds your actual tax liability. This calculator estimates your total federal tax bill but does not know how much your employer has already withheld. To estimate your refund, subtract the tax shown here from the federal withholding on your most recent pay stub or W-2. If withholding is higher, you will likely get a refund; if it is lower, you may owe at filing.

How does the Child Tax Credit work?

The Child Tax Credit provides up to $2,200 per qualifying child under age 17. Up to $1,700 of that may be refundable (the Additional Child Tax Credit) if the credit exceeds your tax liability. Each child needs a valid Social Security number, and the credit phases out above $200,000 of income ($400,000 for joint filers). The refundable part is paid when you file your return, not in your paychecks. This calculator applies a simplified credit model, including an estimated refundable ACTC component based on earned income above $2,500, and does not verify all eligibility rules such as age, relationship, residency or filing-specific limits. Families with three or more children may qualify for a larger refundable amount under an alternative formula that is not modeled here.

What is a W-4 and how does it affect my paycheck?

Form W-4 tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. It replaced the old allowances system — now you directly enter dollar amounts for deductions, credits and extra withholding. If too little is withheld you may owe a penalty at tax time; if too much is withheld you get a larger refund but give the IRS an interest-free loan all year. Use the results from this calculator to check whether your current withholding is on track and update your W-4 accordingly.

What is the federal income tax rate for 2026?

The 2026 federal income tax rates are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37%. The rate that applies to you depends on your taxable income and filing status. For example, a single filer pays 10% only on the first $12,400 of taxable income, with higher rates applying only to income above each bracket threshold.

How do I calculate my paycheck after taxes?

Start with your gross pay, subtract pre-tax deductions like a 401(k) or HSA, then subtract the standard or itemized deduction to find taxable income. Apply the federal brackets to get federal income tax, add Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%), and subtract any state income tax. What's left is your take-home pay. This calculator does all of that automatically as you type.

What is FICA tax?

FICA is the combined Social Security and Medicare payroll tax withheld from wages. Social Security is 6.2% of wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500, and Medicare is 1.45% on all wages, plus an extra 0.9% on high earners. For most employees, FICA totals 7.65% of gross pay.

How much tax do I pay on a $50,000 salary?

A single filer earning $50,000 in 2026 with no pre-tax deductions has a taxable income of $33,900 after the $16,100 standard deduction. Federal income tax is about $3,820, plus $3,100 in Social Security and $725 in Medicare, for a total of roughly $7,645 — take-home pay of about $42,355 per year, or $3,530 per month before any state tax.

How much tax do I pay on a $100,000 salary?

A single filer earning $100,000 in 2026 with no pre-tax deductions has a taxable income of $83,900 after the standard deduction. Federal income tax is about $13,170, plus $6,200 in Social Security and $1,450 in Medicare, for a total of roughly $20,820 — take-home pay of about $79,180 per year, or $6,598 per month before any state tax. Contributing to a 401(k) or HSA would lower these figures.

How much is 2026 federal income tax on $75,000?

A single filer earning $75,000 in 2026 with no pre-tax deductions has taxable income of $58,900 after the $16,100 standard deduction. Federal income tax is approximately $8,398, plus $4,650 in Social Security and $1,088 in Medicare, for a total of roughly $14,136 — take-home pay of about $60,864 per year, or $5,072 per month before state tax.

What is the 2026 tax bracket for married filing jointly?

For 2026, married filing jointly brackets are: 10% up to $24,800; 12% from $24,800 to $100,800; 22% from $100,800 to $211,400; 24% from $211,400 to $403,550; 32% from $403,550 to $512,450; 35% from $512,450 to $768,700; and 37% above $768,700.

How do I calculate my 2026 tax refund?

Subtract your total 2026 federal tax liability (shown by this calculator) from the federal income tax withheld on your W-2 or pay stubs. If withholding exceeds your liability, you get a refund. If it's lower, you owe the difference. Refundable credits like the Additional Child Tax Credit are added on top of any withholding.

Are 401(k) contributions tax-deductible in 2026?

Yes, traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your federal taxable income for 2026. The 2026 employee contribution limit is $24,500. Eligible participants age 50 and older can generally contribute an additional $8,000 (usual combined maximum $32,500); participants ages 60–63 may qualify for a higher $11,250 catch-up limit if their plan allows it. Special rules can apply. However, 401(k) contributions remain subject to Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes. This calculator lets you enter your pre-tax 401(k) amount to see the impact.

What is the difference between gross pay and net pay?

Gross pay is your total earnings before any deductions — your salary or hourly rate times hours worked. Net pay (take-home pay) is what actually lands in your bank account after federal income tax, FICA, state tax, and benefit or retirement deductions. For example, $60,000 gross might become roughly $50,000 net after taxes.

How this conversion works

Converting between hourly pay and annual salary is straightforward once you know the standard multipliers. A full-time job in the US typically means 40 hours per week and 52 weeks per year (including paid time off). Multiply your hourly rate by those two numbers to get your gross annual salary — before any taxes or deductions.

For example, $25/hour × 40 hours × 52 weeks = $52,000 per year. From there you can divide by 12 for monthly pay, by 26 for biweekly paychecks, or by 24 for semi-monthly pay. Each pay period type has a different number of periods per year, which is why the per-check amounts differ even when the annual total stays the same.

Frequently asked questions

$25 an hour is how much a year?

At $25 per hour working a standard full-time schedule of 40 hours per week and 52 weeks per year, your gross annual salary is $52,000. That breaks down to about $4,333 per month, $2,000 per biweekly paycheck, or $1,000 per week before taxes and deductions.

What is the difference between biweekly and semi-monthly pay?

Biweekly means you get paid every two weeks — 26 paychecks per year. Semi-monthly means twice per month — 24 paychecks per year. Biweekly paychecks are slightly smaller but you get two extra per year. For a $52,000 salary, biweekly pay is $2,000 per check while semi-monthly is about $2,167 per check.

How do I convert annual salary to hourly rate?

Divide your annual salary by the total hours you work in a year. For a standard full-time schedule, that's 2,080 hours (40 hours × 52 weeks). For example, $60,000 ÷ 2,080 = $28.85 per hour. If you work a different schedule, adjust the hours per week and weeks per year in this calculator.

How many hours per year is full-time?

The standard US full-time schedule is 40 hours per week × 52 weeks = 2,080 hours per year. Some employers use 50 weeks instead of 52 to account for unpaid time off, which gives 2,000 hours — a common rule of thumb for quick mental math.

How loan payments are calculated

Every fixed-rate loan payment has two parts: principal (the amount you borrowed) and interest (the cost of borrowing). Your monthly payment stays the same, but the split between principal and interest changes over time. Early in the loan, most of each payment goes toward interest. As the balance shrinks, more goes toward principal.

The formula lenders use is: M = P × r × (1+r)^n ÷ ((1+r)^n − 1), where M is the monthly payment, P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (APR ÷ 12), and n is the total number of months. This calculator applies that formula automatically and shows you the full year-by-year breakdown.

Making extra payments directly reduces your principal balance, which means less interest accrues on every future payment. Even $50–$100 extra per month can shave months or years off a loan and save hundreds or thousands in interest.

Frequently asked questions

How is a loan payment calculated?

Lenders use the amortization formula: M = P × r × (1+r)^n ÷ ((1+r)^n − 1). P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (APR divided by 12), and n is the total number of monthly payments. This gives a fixed monthly amount where early payments are mostly interest and later payments are mostly principal.

Does paying extra reduce the total interest on a loan?

Yes, significantly. Extra payments go directly toward your principal balance. Since interest is calculated on the remaining balance, a lower balance means less interest charged every month going forward. Use the "Extra monthly payment" field in this calculator to see exactly how much interest you could save and how much sooner you'd pay off the loan.

What is a good interest rate for a personal loan?

As of 2026, personal loan rates typically range from about 6% to 36% depending on your credit score, income, and loan term. Borrowers with excellent credit (740+) generally see rates under 12%, while those with fair credit may face rates of 20% or more. Auto loans tend to have lower rates, often 5%–12% for new cars.

Should I choose a shorter or longer loan term?

A shorter term means higher monthly payments but much less total interest. A longer term lowers your monthly payment but costs more over the life of the loan. For example, a $25,000 loan at 7.5% costs about $5,057 in interest over 5 years but $8,321 over 8 years. Choose the shortest term whose monthly payment fits comfortably in your budget.

Understanding your mortgage payment

Your total monthly housing payment includes more than just the loan. The PITI acronym covers the four main parts: Principal, Interest, Taxes, and Insurance. Some properties also have HOA dues, and if your down payment is under 20%, you'll likely pay PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance) as well.

Principal and interest are calculated using the same amortization formula as any fixed-rate loan. Property tax is typically 0.5%–2.5% of the home's value per year depending on your county. Home insurance averages $1,200–$2,000 per year for most single-family homes. PMI usually costs 0.3%–1.5% of the loan amount per year until you reach 20% equity.

A general rule of thumb is that your total monthly housing cost should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income (the "front-end ratio"), and all your debt payments combined should stay under 36% (the "back-end ratio"). Lenders use these guidelines — along with your credit score and down payment — to determine how much house you can afford.

Buying a $400,000 home with 20% down ($80,000) means a $320,000 loan. At 6.5% over 30 years, the monthly payment breaks down as:

Frequently asked questions

How much house can I afford?

A common guideline is the 28/36 rule: your monthly housing payment (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) should be at most 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total debt payments (including the mortgage, car loans, credit cards) should stay under 36%. For a $100,000 annual income ($8,333/month), that means a maximum housing payment of about $2,333 per month.

What is PMI and how do I avoid it?

PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance) protects the lender if you default on the loan. It's typically required when your down payment is less than 20% of the home price. PMI usually costs 0.3%–1.5% of the loan amount per year — on a $320,000 loan, that's $960–$4,800 annually. To avoid PMI, put down at least 20%. Once you reach 20% equity through payments or appreciation, you can request to have it removed.

Should I choose a 30-year or 15-year mortgage?

A 15-year mortgage has higher monthly payments but a lower interest rate and dramatically less total interest. A 30-year mortgage offers lower, more manageable payments at a slightly higher rate. For a $320,000 loan at 6.5%, a 15-year term costs about $2,790/month with $182,000 total interest, while a 30-year term costs about $2,023/month but $408,000 total interest. Choose based on your monthly budget and long-term goals.

How much should I put down on a house?

The traditional recommendation is 20% to avoid PMI and get the best rates. However, many lenders offer loans with as little as 3%–5% down. A larger down payment means lower monthly payments, less interest over the life of the loan, and no PMI. A smaller down payment preserves your cash for emergencies, repairs, and other investments. How compound interest grows your savings

Compound interest means you earn interest not just on your original deposit, but also on the interest you've already earned. Over time, this creates a snowball effect where your money grows faster and faster. The two key factors are the interest rate and the compounding frequency — the more often interest is calculated and added, the faster your balance grows.

For example, $5,000 invested at 7% annual return grows to about $9,836 in 10 years with no additional contributions. But if you add just $300 per month, that same account reaches roughly $66,000 in 10 years — about $41,000 of which is pure growth. Time is your most powerful tool: the earlier you start saving, the more compounding works in your favor.

This projection assumes a constant annual return with monthly compounding and end-of-month contributions. Actual savings and investment returns vary by account, fees, taxes, and market conditions.

Frequently asked questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is when you earn interest on both your original deposit and on the interest that has already accumulated. For example, $1,000 at 7% earns $70 in year one. In year two, you earn 7% on $1,070 — $74.90. Over decades, this snowball effect turns small regular contributions into significant wealth. Albert Einstein reportedly called it "the eighth wonder of the world".

How much should I save monthly to reach $100,000?

It depends on your timeline and expected return. At a 7% annual return: to reach $100,000 in 10 years, you'd need to save about $580/month. In 15 years, about $320/month. In 20 years, about $195/month. The longer your timeline, the less you need to contribute each month because compound interest does more of the heavy lifting. Use this calculator to test different scenarios.

What is a good annual return for savings?

It depends on where you put your money. High-yield savings accounts currently offer 4%–5% APY with no risk. CDs offer similar rates for locked-in terms. The S&P 500 has historically returned about 10% annually on average before inflation (roughly 7% after inflation), but with significant year-to-year volatility. For long-term goals (10+ years), investing typically outperforms savings. For short-term goals, stick with insured savings products.

How much of my savings should be in an emergency fund?

Most financial advisors recommend 3–6 months of essential expenses in an easily accessible emergency fund. If your essential monthly costs (rent, food, utilities, transportation, insurance) total $3,000, aim for $9,000–$18,000. Keep this in a high-yield savings account where it's safe, liquid, and earning some interest. Once your emergency fund is fully stocked, redirect monthly contributions toward investing for long-term goals.
